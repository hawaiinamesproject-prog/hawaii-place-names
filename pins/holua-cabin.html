Hōlua Cabin

Maui — Haleakalā National Park

Place: Hōlua Cabin, Haleakalā National Park, Maui

Type: Wilderness cabin / historic shelter / cultural landscape

Story it tells: A remote cabin named for the ancient Hawaiian sport of hōlua sledding, located beneath the steep slopes near Koʻolau Gap.

Hōlua Cabin in Haleakalā National Park. Photo by Forest and Kim Starr, licensed under CC BY 3.0 via Wikimedia Commons.

Hōlua Cabin is a wilderness shelter located on the floor of Haleakalā National Park near Koʻolau Gap. Nestled within native shrubland at roughly 6,900 feet above sea level, it is the most accessible of the park's backcountry cabins, reached by a 3.7-mile hike from the Halemauʻu Trail or a longer trek from Keoneheʻeheʻe, also known as the Sliding Sands Trail.

The cabin takes its name from hōlua, the traditional Hawaiian sport of sled surfing. In heʻe hōlua, riders launched themselves headfirst down steep slopes on long wooden sleds known as papa hōlua. The severe terrain surrounding Koʻolau Gap provided natural routes for sledding. The cabin name preserves a connection between the landscape and one of ancient Hawaiʻi's most distinctive sports.

Before the cabin was built, travelers sheltered nearby in Hōlua Cave, a lava tube located a short walk from the cabin and campgrounds. Positioned beneath the towering walls of Leleiwi Pali and near the entrance to Koʻolau Gap, the area served as an important resting place for those crossing the crater floor. The cave remains one of the lesser-known historic features of the region.

The current cabin was constructed by the Civilian Conservation Corps in 1937 as part of efforts to improve access within the national park. Today, hikers use Hōlua as a base for exploring the crater interior, nearby lava flows, and destinations such as the Silversword Loop, which lies a short distance away along the trail network.

Hōlua Cabin's name recalls ancient Hawaiian athletic traditions, its nearby cave preserves evidence of earlier travel through the crater, and its Civilian Conservation Corps-era construction represents the development of the modern national park.
