Māhāʻulepū Heritage Trail

Kauaʻi — Poʻipū

Place: Māhāʻulepū Heritage Trail, Poʻipū, Kauaʻi

Type: Coastal trail / cultural landscape / archaeological area

Story it tells: A coastal trail preserving one of Kauaʻi's most significant cultural landscapes, where traditional place names, archaeological sites, burial dunes, and oral traditions reveal centuries of Hawaiian history.

The Māhāʻulepū coastline along the Heritage Trail on Kauaʻi. Photo by cogdogblog, licensed under CC BY 2.0 via Wikimedia Commons.

The Māhāʻulepū Heritage Trail follows approximately two miles of Kauaʻi's rugged south shore between Punahoa Point and Paʻōʻō Point. Unlike much of Poʻipū's coastline, this stretch remains largely undeveloped, allowing visitors to experience dramatic limestone cliffs, sand dunes, tide pools, and broad ocean vistas as they have appeared for generations. The trail also passes through one of Hawaiʻi's most culturally and archaeologically significant landscapes.

The name Māhāʻulepū is commonly interpreted as "falling together" or "scattered together." Traditional accounts associate the name with a great battle in which invading warriors were defeated, although the exact event remains uncertain. Some oral traditions place the conflict centuries before Western contact, while others connect it to later conflicts involving Kauaʻi's ruling chiefs. Archaeologists have not confirmed a specific battle, but the place name preserves a longstanding association between the landscape and memories of warfare.

The area contains an extraordinary concentration of archaeological resources. Ancient Hawaiian petroglyphs, habitation sites, caves, agricultural features, and carved stones have all been documented along or near the trail. The coastal dunes are especially significant because they contain numerous Native Hawaiian burials. The massive dune known as ʻĀweoweo has been described as one of the largest burial dunes in the Pacific, and human remains are occasionally exposed as winds uncover portions of the ancient sand deposits. Today, newly discovered burials are protected and respectfully reinterred through established protocols with the Kauaʻi-Niʻihau Island Burial Council.

Nineteenth-century visitors described human remains scattered throughout the dunes, though the reasons remain debated. Some Hawaiians explained that the dunes served as traditional burial grounds because the soft sand made excavation easier. Others maintained that the remains represented warriors killed during a great battle that gave Māhāʻulepū its name. Whether the dunes reflect ordinary burial practices, warfare, or a combination of both, they remain places of deep cultural importance that continue to be treated with great respect.

Today the Māhāʻulepū Heritage Trail offers more than scenic hiking. It provides access to one of the last largely undeveloped stretches of Kauaʻi's south coast, where geology, archaeology, oral tradition, and Native Hawaiian history converge across the rugged landscape.
