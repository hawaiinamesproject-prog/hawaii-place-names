Rice Street

Kauaʻi — Līhuʻe

Place: Rice Street, Līhuʻe, Kauaʻi

Type: Historic street / civic and commercial corridor

Namesake: William Hyde Rice (1846–1924), Kauaʻi rancher, politician, and Hawaiian-language scholar

Story it tells: How Līhuʻe's former Government Road became Rice Street, preserving the name of one of Kauaʻi's most influential figures through the political and economic transformations of the late Hawaiian Kingdom and plantation era.

William Hyde Rice, namesake of Rice Street in Līhuʻe. Photo via Wikimedia Commons, Public Domain.

Rice Street is the historic main street of Līhuʻe, Kauaʻi's civic and commercial center. For much of its early history, its name was Government Road and it was a dirt-and-gravel road that supported early plantation transport between Līhuʻe with Nāwiliwili Harbor.

In August 1938, the Kauaʻi Board of Supervisors officially renamed Government Road in honor of William Hyde Rice. Rice had deep roots in Līhuʻe and became one of the most prominent figures on nineteenth-century Kauaʻi. Born in 1846 on Oʻahu, he arrived on Kauaʻi as a child after his father became manager of Līhuʻe Plantation. Rice later established Kīpū Plantation and Līhuʻe Ranch, accumulated extensive acreage, and became a major figure in the island's ranching and agricultural economy.

Rice was fluent in Hawaiian and developed close relationships with Hawaiian communities and aliʻi. He collected stories and traditions that were eventually published as Hawaiian Legends. His familiarity with Hawaiian language and culture made him unusual among the missionary-descended businessmen who became increasingly powerful during the late Hawaiian Kingdom.

His political career also placed him within some of the most consequential changes in Hawaiian history. Rice served for years in the Hawaiian Kingdom legislature and became governor of Kauaʻi under Queen Liliʻuokalani in 1891. But he was also associated with the political movement that forced King Kalākaua to accept the 1887 Bayonet Constitution, which sharply reduced the monarch's authority and changed voting qualifications. Following the overthrow of the Hawaiian Kingdom in 1893, Rice participated in the government that followed, serving as a delegate to the constitutional convention of the Republic of Hawaiʻi and later as a senator.

The street that eventually received his name had meanwhile become the center of a changing Līhuʻe. Government Road was once traveled by plantation wagons, workers, merchants, and increasingly automobiles. Sugar fields and plantation facilities surrounded portions of the corridor, while worker housing, small stores, restaurants, and other businesses developed alongside it.

Government infrastructure also became increasingly visible along the road. In 1912, Kauaʻi County acquired land from Līhuʻe Plantation for a permanent civic center, and the County Building was completed the following year. Other institutions and commercial buildings changed the plantation-era road into the civic and commercial spine of Līhuʻe. By the late 1930s, improvements to streets, sidewalks, and the surrounding town accompanied the transition from Government Road to Rice Street.

Rice Street remains one of Līhuʻe's principal corridors today. Recent revitalization efforts have emphasized pedestrian spaces, local businesses, housing, and community events, attempting to strengthen the street as a town center rather than simply a route through Līhuʻe. Its name still points backward to the plantation and political world from which modern Līhuʻe emerged.

William Hyde Rice left a complicated legacy. He spoke Hawaiian, recorded Hawaiian traditions, and served as a royal governor, while also participating in political changes that weakened and ultimately replaced the government he had served. Rice Street carries the name of a man whose life crossed the Hawaiian Kingdom, the plantation economy, and the political transformation of Hawaiʻi itself. It remembers a man whose loyalties crossed both sides during challenging times.
