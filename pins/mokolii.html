Mokoliʻi

Oʻahu

Place: Mokoliʻi, Kāneʻohe Bay, Oʻahu

Type: Offshore islet / cultural landscape

Story it tells: A small offshore island whose Hawaiian name, mythology, and later nickname reflect different ways people have interpreted Hawaiʻi’s landscapes over time.

Mokoliʻi rising offshore beneath the cliffs of Kualoa on Oʻahu’s windward coast. Photo by Eric Tessmer, licensed under CC BY 4.0 via Wikimedia Commons.

Mokoliʻi is a small basalt islet located offshore of Kualoa along the windward coast of Oʻahu. The Hawaiian name is commonly translated as “little lizard,” with moko referring to a lizard or dragon-like creature and liʻi meaning small. The islet was once connected to the larger basalt ridges of Oʻahu before erosion separated it from the shoreline approximately six thousand years ago.

In Hawaiian tradition, Mokoliʻi is tied to stories involving Hiʻiaka, the sister of Pele and an important figure in Hawaiian mythology. According to Hawaiian tradition, Hiʻiaka killed a giant moʻo near Kualoa during her journey across the islands. Its severed tail was thrown into the ocean by Hiʻiaka and it became Mokoliʻi, while nearby Hakipuʻu was said to represent the broken spine of the moʻo. These traditions helped connect the landscape itself to larger stories about movement, conflict, and the akua (gods).

Today, many people know the islet by the nickname “Chinaman’s Hat,” a reference to its resemblance to an Asian conical hat when viewed from shore. The nickname became widely used during the plantation and territorial eras, reflecting the growing Chinese presence in Hawaiʻi during the 19th and early 20th centuries. Nearby places such as Mākao in Hauʻula preserve related histories of Chinese migration and settlement on Oʻahu’s windward coast. At the same time, some Native Hawaiian scholars and cultural practitioners have criticized the nickname for overshadowing the Hawaiian name and the traditions connected to Mokoliʻi itself.

Mokoliʻi remains one of the most recognizable landmarks in Kāneʻohe Bay and is now part of Kualoa Regional Park. Visitors regularly kayak, swim, and hike to the small island, though its visibility from shore continues to make it as much a symbolic landmark as a physical destination. Rising sharply from the water beneath the cliffs of Kualoa, Mokoliʻi reflects the layered ways Hawaiʻi’s landscapes have been named, interpreted, renamed, and remembered over time.
